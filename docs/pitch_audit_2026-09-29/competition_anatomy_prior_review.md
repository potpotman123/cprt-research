# HFAC x Citadel 2026 — Competition anatomy

**Research cutoff:** 2026-08-31, 4:00 p.m. ET
**Evidence standard:** placements are stated only where a competition sponsor, school, or the Culverhouse archive identifies the result. Later share-price performance was not used to grade a pitch.

## Executive finding

The best public competition pitches do not win by finding the most impressive business or the most elaborate model. They identify one measurable expectation the market appears to hold, produce a reproducible piece of evidence that contradicts it, translate that evidence into an estimate change, and name the event that should reveal the error. The most transferable architecture is:

> **Expectation → differentiated evidence → KPI delta → earnings delta → dated catalyst → probability-weighted payoff → falsifier.**

That chain is substantive. Polished pages, large TAMs, and crowded logo charts are presentation devices unless each changes an operating assumption.

## Ten characteristics shared by the strongest pitches

1. They begin with a falsifiable expectations gap, not a company overview.
2. They express the thesis in no more than three operating drivers.
3. At least one piece of evidence is reproducible from public information.
4. Research is tied to a defined sample, date range and bias check.
5. The operating bridge identifies which quarterly estimate changes first.
6. Catalysts reveal a KPI; they are not generic items such as “earnings.”
7. Reverse valuation exposes what the price already assumes.
8. Base, bull and bear cases change operations separately from the multiple.
9. The downside case is argued as seriously as the upside case.
10. The pitch includes an observable kill criterion and a monitoring plan.

## Ten common failure modes

1. “Great company” or “bad company” presented as a security thesis.
2. Large TAM without a measurable adoption denominator.
3. Valuation-only long or short with no estimate-changing event.
4. A catalyst outside the competition's 3–12 month horizon.
5. Consensus estimates cited without a point-in-time source.
6. Channel checks with no sample design, date, question wording or bias discussion.
7. A model whose key assumption is disconnected from the thesis evidence.
8. Risks described generically, with no falsification thresholds.
9. Probability-weighted value that hides unrealistic multiple expansion or compression.
10. Legal, regulatory or M&A outcomes carrying the entire expected value.

## Substance versus presentation

| Substantive technique | Merely presentational unless it changes an assumption |
|---|---|
| Reverse DCF / implied operating case | A football field of peer multiples |
| Reproducible store, product, price, job-posting or rollout dataset | A logo mosaic or generic market map |
| Cohort or unit-economics analysis | A large TAM circle |
| KPI-to-quarter earnings bridge | A dense historical chart with no forecast implication |
| Dated catalyst with expected versus observed KPI | “Upcoming earnings” listed as a catalyst |
| Explicit kill criteria and downside liquidity analysis | Boilerplate risk factors |

## What fits in two pages

- Header: direction, price, target, horizon and one-sentence variant.
- Three numbered pillars, each with one chart or numeric proof and one earnings implication.
- One compact expectations / estimate bridge.
- One catalyst strip with dates and reveal metrics.
- One probability-weighted valuation table and one risk / kill-criteria box.
- Footnotes limited to the sources that prove those claims; the model and source log carry the audit trail.

## Likely Citadel-style challenges

- What is genuinely non-consensus, and what proves that it is not already in the price?
- Which single input creates most of the upside or downside?
- What happens with no multiple change?
- Why will the evidence become visible within 12 months?
- How does the thesis survive the strongest opposing interpretation of the same data?
- Is the “research edge” reproducible, current and large enough to move an estimate?
- What is the exact exit condition if the thesis is wrong?
