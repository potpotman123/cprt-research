## Thesis 1 — modeling

We model coverage explicitly rather than as a claims haircut. Insured total losses equal the fleet roll's crashes multiplied by a physical-damage coverage take-up rate: the share of drivers with insurance (IRC: 87.6% in 2017, 84.6% in 2023) times the share of insured drivers carrying collision (NAIC via III: 76%, 77%, 77% for 2021–23). The rate fell 2.2% from 2017 to 2023, all of it from drivers leaving insurance; collision take-up held through the premium spike. The driver behind it is the premium burden, the BLS motor-vehicle-insurance CPI divided by average hourly earnings: 0.83 in 2019, 0.87 in 2023 when households were leaving, 1.00 at the 2025 peak, and 0.96 so far in 2026 even after a 5% premium decline. Relative to pay, insurance is still 10% dearer than when drivers walked away, before any lapse surcharge.

Three forward paths run FQ1 FY27–FQ4 FY28. *Sticky* holds coverage at today's level; this is our thesis and our base case. *Street-implied* reverse-solves the recovery JPMorgan's FY27 legacy service revenue of $4,061m requires: +3.5% insured volume in one year, more than the whole measured 2017–23 decline. *Premium-response* is the bull's mechanism with hysteresis: coverage returns at the 2017–23 response (0.37) only once the burden falls back below its 2023 level. On the observed BLS trend (premiums −5% a year, earnings +3%) that crossing comes in FQ1 FY28, so recovery begins a year after the Street needs it and FY27 is unchanged. On an S&P-consistent path it does not come in the horizon: S&P projects auto underwriting deteriorating from a 94.5 combined ratio in 2025 to 97.1 in 2026 and 98.9 in 2027, breaching breakeven in 2028, which points to renewed rate increases rather than a sustained decline. The sticky path is worth $104m of FY27 legacy service revenue against JPM (−2.6%). (Tabs: D Coverage, E2 rows 103–131, Scenarios cases 0–1b.)

## Thesis 2 — modeling

We convert aftermarket adoption into a comparable-repair discount through a measured parts basket: CCC's 2025 replacement-part dollar shares (OEM 66.8%, aftermarket 22.7%, recycled 10.5%) at relative prices of 1.00 for OEM and 0.73 for aftermarket and recycled at the anchor (Mitchell's matched 2016 aftermarket discounts, mean 27%; recycled set equal because trade sources overlap), with aftermarket the only source whose price is not rising: PartsTrader's Collision Parts Price Index has new OEM up 4.3% since the April 2025 tariffs and recycled tracking it, because recyclers price off the OE list, while aftermarket held under 1% as suppliers absorbed the tariff, a relative-price drift of 3.3 points a year that we carry through FY28 (0.73 → 0.71 → 0.68 against OEM and recycled). With parts at 40% of the repair bill (CCC range 36–44%), the observed mix trend (aftermarket +1.7 points a year, recycled −0.2) plus the drift lowers the whole bill 0.5% by FQ4 FY27 and 1.0% by FQ4 FY28; that passes through the damage-selection engine's total-loss threshold (repair cost versus value less expected salvage) at the calibrated near-threshold response of about 2% of total losses per 1% of bill, so insured total-loss units fall 0.9% by FQ4 FY27 and 1.9% by FQ4 FY28, applied to the quarterly units chain. The displaced recycled share reaches price through dismantler bids, scaled from the engine's solved endpoint (−0.2% on auction price by FQ4 FY27), and price reaches revenue per unit through the posted buyer-fee grid (elasticity 0.42) and the 4% seller commission, so RPU falls about 0.1%; units times RPU gives −$19m of FY27 and −$58m of FY28 legacy service revenue against the known-facts case, with the memo's 5.5-point stress case, the engine's assumed-basket parity (1.03% bill, −2.06% units) and the three dismantler-bid coefficients (25% donor exposure, 1.5× contribution-to-bid, 50% transmission) shown beside it as labelled sensitivities, plus a no-displacement countercase. The drift is one year of index data under tariffs and is incremental to a reference that holds general repair-cost inflation neutral; both are stated on the tab.

## Thesis 3 — modeling

Copart's share of the insured total-loss pool is the sum over ten carriers of pool weight times allocation to Copart. Weights are premium shares frozen at FQ4 FY26 (a proxy, labelled). Progressive's realised move, 25% to 5% over FQ3 FY26–FQ1 FY27, is fitted to the FQ4 FY26 print and always on; its FQ4 FY26 effect is derived in-model at −5.0% of service revenue. Further moves are probability-weighted from dated expert evidence: State Farm −5 points at 45% from FQ3 FY27, regional carriers −5 points at 45% from FQ1 FY27, each phased over its ramp; the GEICO win (+12 points at 60%) is off in the bear variant. Share falls from 56.6% at FQ4 FY26 to 55.3% at FQ4 FY27, worth −$26m of FY27 legacy service revenue against the known-facts case. Seller concessions (20% of a 4% commission on a chosen share of volume) are a toggle, zero by default. The parity evidence sits beside the model with sources: IAA 14,803 acres and 333 locations (RB Global 10-K) versus Copart's 286 locations; insurer contracts cancellable on 30–90 days' notice; a former Progressive executive on RB Global's board; Copart's FY2026 10-K newly describing seller arrangements as non-exclusive and terminable on limited notice. ## Modelling and Valuation (draft continuation, 2 October 2026)

We unconditionally acknowledge that no bottom-up model of Copart can be more precise than what Copart discloses, which is very little: no absolute unit counts, no carrier mix, no fee mix, no coverage data. We therefore did not build the model to look precise. We built it so that every number can be traced to a filing, a dataset or a labelled assumption, and so that the three theses run on one common base where their interactions are counted once.

The model is a claim-to-sale chain, quarterly from FQ1 FY26 to FQ4 FY28, in six engines that feed one revenue page (the RPM) and then our DCF. The fleet roll starts from every year's US light-vehicle sales by body since 1970 (Ward's via ORNL, FRED for 2022–25) and ages them with EPA survival schedules stretched to the IHS/Polk 2013 census and 2024 vehicle counts; its one out-of-sample test is that it reproduces the 2020–25 swing in CCC's claim mix (cars −10 points of claims, utility vehicles +10) without being fitted to it. Claims propensity by age and body and total-loss frequency across sixteen body-by-age cells come from CCC's 2020–25 data; thesis 1's coverage take-up and premium-burden threshold sit on top (IRC, NAIC via III, BLS CPI-U and Establishment Survey). Carrier allocation is pool weight times Copart share by carrier, with Progressive's runoff fitted to the FQ4 FY26 print and the thesis-3 moves probability-weighted from dated expert evidence (RB Global's FY2025 10-K and 2026 proxy supply the parity facts). The aftermarket engine converts CCC source shares, Mitchell's matched discounts and PartsTrader's price index into a repair-bill discount and runs it through the damage-selection threshold, repair cost against value less expected salvage, at the response the engine's calibration implies. Prices and fees apply Copart's posted buyer-fee grid, transcribed in September 2026, a 4% seller commission and attached services to a historical ledger anchored to the 8-K quarterly service revenue; international carries the disclosed +3.5% fee RPU forward, and ACV is excluded so the perimeter matches the benchmark.

Validation is live inside the workbook rather than asserted here. Thirteen checks recalculate with every change: the FY26 ledger ties to the 8-Ks within $0.1m, units and shares obey their identities, and the known-facts case lands within $3m of our Python engine's reference-plus-allocation case while the thesis-2 effect lands within $6m of the engine's aftermarket effect. Every typed input, 107 of them, carries an evidence label (verified, measured, fitted, calibrated, assumed or unverified) and a source note on a Sources tab generated from the cells; 42 data points the engines use live on a facts tab with file, date and URL. The coefficients that remain assumptions, the dismantler-bid trio, service adoption, the 90% insurance share of US service dollars, are run as visible sensitivities, and the no-displacement countercase shows thesis 2's price channel can be close to zero.

Seven cases run on the common base. The Street-implied case reverse-solves the coverage recovery JPMorgan's FY27 legacy service revenue of $4,061m requires; the known-facts case, with the Progressive loss lapping and coverage flat, lands at $3,957m, a $104m (−2.6%) gap that is thesis 1's value. Thesis 2 adds −$19m, thesis 3 −$26m, and all three together reach $3,912m, −3.7% against JPMorgan and about −$200m against the revenue the market itself implies at today's price, where our reverse DCF's 4.0% CAGR coincides with the CapIQ consensus of $4,861m. JPMorgan is the comparator for the driver-level contest only because it is the one broker that writes down a service-revenue line; Barclays, the Street's Underweight, carries the same FY27 revenue within $14m, so the disagreement among brokers is about multiple and margin, not revenue. The selected case feeds revenue, units and revenue per unit into our DCF unchanged in structure; the price target follows from there.
