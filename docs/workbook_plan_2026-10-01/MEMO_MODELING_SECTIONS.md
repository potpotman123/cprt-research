## Thesis 1 — modeling

We model coverage explicitly rather than as a claims haircut. Insured total losses equal the fleet roll's crashes multiplied by a physical-damage coverage take-up rate: the share of drivers with insurance (IRC: 87.6% in 2017, 84.6% in 2023) times the share of insured drivers carrying collision (NAIC via III: 76%, 77%, 77% for 2021–23). The rate fell 2.2% from 2017 to 2023, all of it from drivers leaving insurance; collision take-up held through the premium spike. The driver behind it is the premium burden, the BLS motor-vehicle-insurance CPI divided by average hourly earnings: 0.83 in 2019, 0.87 in 2023 when households were leaving, 1.00 at the 2025 peak, and 0.96 so far in 2026 even after a 5% premium decline. Relative to pay, insurance is still 10% dearer than when drivers walked away, before any lapse surcharge.

Three forward paths run FQ1 FY27–FQ4 FY28. *Sticky* holds coverage at today's level; this is our thesis and our base case. *Street-implied* reverse-solves the recovery JPMorgan's FY27 legacy service revenue of $4,061m requires: +3.5% insured volume in one year, more than the whole measured 2017–23 decline. *Premium-response* is the bull's mechanism with hysteresis: coverage returns at the 2017–23 response (0.37) only once the burden falls back below its 2023 level. On the observed BLS trend (premiums −5% a year, earnings +3%) that crossing comes in FQ1 FY28, so recovery begins a year after the Street needs it and FY27 is unchanged. On an S&P-consistent path it does not come in the horizon: S&P projects auto underwriting deteriorating from a 94.5 combined ratio in 2025 to 97.1 in 2026 and 98.9 in 2027, breaching breakeven in 2028, which points to renewed rate increases rather than a sustained decline. The sticky path is worth $104m of FY27 legacy service revenue against JPM (−2.6%). (Tabs: D Coverage, E2 rows 103–131, Scenarios cases 0–1b.)

## Thesis 2 — modeling

We convert aftermarket adoption into a comparable-repair discount through a measured parts basket: CCC's 2025 replacement-part dollar shares (OEM 66.8%, aftermarket 22.7%, recycled 10.5%) at relative prices of 1.00 for OEM and 0.73 for aftermarket and recycled at the anchor (Mitchell's matched 2016 aftermarket discounts, mean 27%; recycled set equal because trade sources overlap), with aftermarket the only source whose price is not rising: PartsTrader's Collision Parts Price Index has new OEM up 4.3% since the April 2025 tariffs and recycled tracking it, because recyclers price off the OE list, while aftermarket held under 1% as suppliers absorbed the tariff, a relative-price drift of 3.3 points a year that we carry through FY28 (0.73 → 0.71 → 0.68 against OEM and recycled). With parts at 40% of the repair bill (CCC range 36–44%), the observed mix trend (aftermarket +1.7 points a year, recycled −0.2) plus the drift lowers the whole bill 0.5% by FQ4 FY27 and 1.0% by FQ4 FY28; that passes through the damage-selection engine's total-loss threshold (repair cost versus value less expected salvage) at the calibrated near-threshold response of about 2% of total losses per 1% of bill, so insured total-loss units fall 0.9% by FQ4 FY27 and 1.9% by FQ4 FY28, applied to the quarterly units chain. The displaced recycled share reaches price through dismantler bids, scaled from the engine's solved endpoint (−0.2% on auction price by FQ4 FY27), and price reaches revenue per unit through the posted buyer-fee grid (elasticity 0.42) and the 4% seller commission, so RPU falls about 0.1%; units times RPU gives −$19m of FY27 and −$58m of FY28 legacy service revenue against the known-facts case, with the memo's 5.5-point stress case, the engine's assumed-basket parity (1.03% bill, −2.06% units) and the three dismantler-bid coefficients (25% donor exposure, 1.5× contribution-to-bid, 50% transmission) shown beside it as labelled sensitivities, plus a no-displacement countercase. The drift is one year of index data under tariffs and is incremental to a reference that holds general repair-cost inflation neutral; both are stated on the tab.

## Thesis 3 — modeling

Copart's share of the insured total-loss pool is the sum over ten carriers of pool weight times allocation to Copart. Weights are premium shares frozen at FQ4 FY26 (a proxy, labelled). Progressive's realised move, 25% to 5% over FQ3 FY26–FQ1 FY27, is fitted to the FQ4 FY26 print and always on; its FQ4 FY26 effect is derived in-model at −5.0% of service revenue. Further moves are probability-weighted from dated expert evidence: State Farm −5 points at 45% from FQ3 FY27, regional carriers −5 points at 45% from FQ1 FY27, each phased over its ramp; the GEICO win (+12 points at 60%) is off in the bear variant. Share falls from 56.6% at FQ4 FY26 to 55.3% at FQ4 FY27, worth −$26m of FY27 legacy service revenue against the known-facts case. Seller concessions (20% of a 4% commission on a chosen share of volume) are a toggle, zero by default. The parity evidence sits beside the model with sources: IAA 14,803 acres and 333 locations (RB Global 10-K) versus Copart's 286 locations; insurer contracts cancellable on 30–90 days' notice; a former Progressive executive on RB Global's board; Copart's FY2026 10-K newly describing seller arrangements as non-exclusive and terminable on limited notice. (Tabs: E3 rows 6–96, D Carriers.)
