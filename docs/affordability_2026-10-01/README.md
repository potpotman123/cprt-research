# Copart affordability and loan payoff assessment

As of 1 October 2026. The 30 September Kyle Krumlauf call strengthens the economic explanation for claims selection and an aging fleet. It does **not** establish a new, dated decline in Copart units from pandemic loan maturities. Payoff removes a lender's coverage requirement but also releases monthly cash flow. The timing is spread across origination cohorts and loan terms. The model now has a separate, explicitly hypothetical coverage-loss sensitivity; the central forecast is unchanged because incremental coverage attrition has not been measured.

## What the three supplied sources support

**Experian Q3 2025 Automotive Market Trends, PDF pages 7 and 16–17.** The U.S./Puerto Rico light-duty registered fleet is 295.9 million, versus 292.1 million a year earlier. It reports 16.3 million new registrations, 12.5 million vehicles leaving operation and 39.7 million used vehicles changing owners over the preceding twelve months. Thus fewer recent births can coexist with a growing fleet; and vehicle model year is not loan origination year. Used transactions can create new liens on old cars. Page 17 shows 104.1 million vehicles in the six-to-twelve-model-year aftermarket group (35.2%), down from 105.6 million in 2024. This category measures a mechanical service/parts opportunity, not collision aftermarket penetration or insurance coverage. Page 17 was visually checked. Do not substitute the report's 347.1 million U.S./Canada all-motorized-vehicle count for U.S. light-duty exposure.

[Experian report](https://www.experian.com/content/dam/marketing/na/automotive/quarterly-webinars/market-trends/experian-market-trends-q3-2025.pdf)

**ABPA Totaled, May 2026, PDF pages 24–36 and 55–58.** The useful addition is the experience after a total loss: replacement debt, depleted savings, vehicle downgrades, disrupted mobility and extra work. These accounts help explain why consumers may resist settlements and try to preserve transportation. The study is 30 screened, roughly thirty-minute interviews with people who experienced a total loss within four years. It expressly does not estimate population prevalence; financial and claim records were not independently verified. ABPA commissioned it and advocates parts-market reforms. Its introductory claim of rising repair costs and lower values tends to increase total losses, which can support salvage supply. Consumer hardship is not itself evidence of lower Copart throughput. Nor do selected accounts demonstrate a change in post-payoff coverage retention.

[Report and methods](https://www.autobpa.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ABPA-Consumer-Research-Report-2026-1.pdf)

**iSeeCars 2026 depreciation study.** It reports five-year depreciation of 41.8%, versus 45.6% in 2025; SUVs 44.9%, trucks 34.2%, hybrids 35.4%, EVs 57.2%. Thus SUVs as a broad group retain less value than the overall average; the study does not justify treating every crossover as a superior-value vehicle. Methodology uses over 950,000 five-year-old vehicles from March 2025–February 2026, average asking prices and original MSRP adjusted to 2026 dollars, with model exclusions. This is neither actual borrower purchase price including markups nor loan balance nor Copart salvage hammer price. Comparing annual five-year depreciation estimates is not the return on a fixed vehicle portfolio. Stronger retained retail values could lower total-loss propensity but support recovery values; net service revenue is ambiguous. The current result challenges an automatic assumption that used values must keep falling.

## Loan timing and the economic mechanism

The transcript says **70 months**, approximately six years, not seven months. The immediate-cliff interpretation comes from the interviewer's follow-up; Kyle does not supply a vintage forecast.

Experian's 1 December 2022 financing release reports new-loan average terms of 69.51 months in Q3 2021 and 69.73 in Q3 2022; used-loan terms 66.97 and 68.08. New-loan APR rose from 4.09% to 5.16% and average payment from $618 to $700; used APR from 8.12% to 9.34%, payments $472 to $525. These are cross-sectional means, not one representative loan or the term distribution. They distinguish inventory/price pressure from financing-rate pressure. A supply shortfall can raise prices and constrain sales; affordability should not be presented as the sole original cause of pandemic production declines.

Edmunds' June 2020 new-finance averages were 69.9 months, 4.2% APR and a $568 payment; 19.4% of financed new purchases carried zero-percent offers. This confirms roughly-six-year terms but also shows why the initial pandemic cohort should not be conflated with later high-price/high-rate borrowers. Different vendors' averages are not a matched longitudinal panel.

[Experian historical financing release](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2022/used-vehicle-finance-market-begins-to-level-out-as-used-vehicle-loans-see-smaller-year-over-year-increases-in-q3-2022)

These are contractual calendar calculations for January–December originations, assuming first payment one month later and no early exit or term change. They are not volumes. Early repayment, trade-ins, repossession, total loss, refinancing and extensions alter the surviving schedule. Sixty-month 2021 loans could end in 2026, but the average near seventy months does not put all high-price lending into today's cohort. Copart FY27 runs August 2026–July 2027, so part of the 2021 roughly-six-year cohort overlaps it; much of 2022 belongs later. No evidence here establishes an unusually large payoff wave versus the normal rolling flow of maturities.

For a standard fully amortizing loan, payoff ends payments; it does not create a balloon bill. A purely illustrative $40,000 loan at 6% over 72 months costs $662.92 monthly, with $200 interest and $462.92 principal in month one, and approximately zero principal left after the final payment. Therefore even the general claim that early payments are mostly interest is not universally true. Negative equity before maturity matters for trade-ins and total-loss settlements, but an on-schedule fully paid-off loan has no remaining loan balance. Leases, arrears and balloon structures require separate treatment.

Progressive explicitly confirms that after payoff an owner may remove collision/comprehensive coverage. Liability requirements remain a separate question. The source establishes permission and decision factors, not actual cancellation rates.

[Progressive on insurance after payoff](https://www.progressive.com/answers/insurance-for-paid-off-vehicle/)

The bearish chain is: surviving loan payoff → excess physical-damage coverage cancellation → fewer otherwise insured total-loss assignments → some vehicles fail to reach Copart by another route. A not-at-fault loss may still be paid through the other driver's liability policy; uninsured damaged cars can also reach salvage through non-insurer sellers. Opposing mechanisms are payment relief allowing coverage retention, continued ownership of older vehicles with high total-loss propensity, eventual financed replacement restoring coverage, and fewer lienholders potentially reducing processing friction. These are competing hypotheses, not quantified offsets.

## What changed in the model

The saved reference already combines age-related coverage, driving and filing propensity. Applying another full age-based coverage haircut would double count. The new package at `model/loan_payoff_2026-10-01/` treats **only an incremental deviation** from that reference as a coverage sensitivity. It leaves the forecast coefficient at zero pending evidence.

For the simplified uniform sensitivity:

`Net unit shortfall = payoff cohort share of baseline prospective Copart insurance total losses × excess coverage attrition × (1 − alternate-route recapture)`.

The first denominator is expected Copart insurance total-loss units, not all vehicles or loan count. It requires survival, coverage and loss-risk weights before it can be estimated from originations. Recapture is an assumed net unit offset; the illustration assigns recaptured units baseline-equivalent economics. A calibrated implementation would separately model collision/comprehensive, third-party liability, age/body mix, seller route, revenue per unit and sale lag.

These are **unestimated, flat full-year shocks**, not a payoff schedule or predictions. No new price shock is included. In the nonfiling example Q1 reported total-loss frequency rises from 23.11% to 25.04% while units and revenue remain unchanged. The identity separates loss of repairable-claim denominator from loss of salvage-producing exposure.

The earlier reverse stress required a 6.46% terminal unit shortfall plus an assumed price path to produce a 3% miss against JPM's $4,061m legacy-service estimate. If this loan mechanism alone had to supply that terminal unit reduction, a hypothetical 10% exposed unit weight and 25% recapture would require **86.1% excess coverage attrition**; with 20% exposure, **43.0%**. With only 5% exposure, the requirement is impossible even without recapture. Those are required magnitudes, not evidence; the old ramp and price decline are not validated by this call. The flat annual cases above cannot be substituted directly into the old quarterly ramp.

Arithmetic checks pass: historical calibration unchanged, coverage multipliers reconcile to units, nonfiling changes only the reported-claim denominator, and the original reference hash is unchanged. These checks validate implementation, not behavioral assumptions.

## Pitch decision and the missing observation

The strongest candidate thesis is **insured salvage exposure can deteriorate even while reported total-loss frequency rises**. That is more specific than “old cars are bad for Copart.” The call adds support for the mechanism and a potential financing calendar; it does not yet establish the magnitude, novelty relative to analyst expectations, or a dated surprise. Existing public claims work already discusses affordability and selection.

The decisive dataset would follow financed vehicles through lien payoff and the next policy renewal: original vintage/term, actual payoff and prior exits, vehicle age/value, coverage before/after, earned vehicle exposure and claims, with a comparable still-financed group. Then measure which missing insurer losses reappear in third-party claims or non-insurance auctions. CCC claims alone miss cars that never file; insurer policy/lienholder records are a more direct source of the missing behavior. Renewal effects can also lag payoff, and auction sales lag losses.

A focused follow-up to Kyle would ask for (1) any shareable coverage/lien-status analysis or a source that observes policy exposure, (2) a definition and series for the drivable-total-loss remark, and (3) matched alternative-part savings, separately aftermarket and recycled, controlling for vehicle, part and geography. No outreach has been sent. Until those observations exist, keep the mechanism as a conditional bear case and do not label a 2026 loan maturity wave an established catalyst.
